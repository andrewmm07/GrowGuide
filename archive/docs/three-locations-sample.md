# THREE LOCATIONS TEST — January through March

---

## BLACKMANS BAY, TASMANIA (7052)

### JANUARY

Summer is properly established now. Tomatoes, beans, cucumbers and zucchini should be producing heavily in any reasonably sunny, sheltered garden. Pick constantly. A neglected bean or oversized zucchini slows the whole plant down.

There is still time for late tomatoes from strong seedlings, particularly in warm coastal gardens. Capsicums and eggplants become less certain from this point onward unless you have excellent sun, protection from wind, or the help of a greenhouse.

At the same time, the autumn garden begins now, not later. Sow carrots, beetroot, parsnips, broccoli, kale, cabbage, silverbeet and turnips while the soil still carries summer warmth. Lettuce seed often struggles in heat. Chill it first or sow in shade during the cooler part of the day.

Water deeply in the morning rather than little and often. Dry soil followed by sudden soaking stresses plants badly in Tasmanian summer winds. Mulch heavily while the ground is still warm and moist underneath.

### FEBRUARY

The garden still looks and feels summery, though the light begins to soften by month's end. Nights cool slightly, mornings arrive later, and the frantic pace of early summer eases just enough to think ahead.

Plant out brassicas started in January. Broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower and kale establish beautifully in warm late-summer soil. Continue sowing beetroot, carrots, silverbeet, Asian greens and lettuce for autumn harvests. Coriander becomes much easier now as temperatures settle.

Tomatoes, beans and cucumbers are often at their best this month, but hot northerly winds can still arrive suddenly and dry beds fast. Deep watering before heat arrives is far more effective than trying to rescue wilted plants afterward.

By the end of February, observant gardeners begin preparing mentally for autumn, even while the tomatoes are still ripening.

### MARCH

Autumn arrives quickly in southern Tasmania. Warm afternoons still appear, but the nights cool sharply and the first light frosts are possible late in the month, especially in still weather.

Plant garlic now for harvest in summer. Early in the month there is still time for lettuce, celery, kale and silverbeet seedlings to establish well. Sow spinach, peas, broad beans and Asian greens as the soil cools. Root crops such as carrots and beetroot are best sown sooner rather than later while there is still warmth left in the ground.

Growth begins slowing noticeably. Plants no longer race away as they did in December. Mulch now becomes as much about frost buffering as moisture retention. Keep fleece or cloches nearby for young seedlings if a sudden cold change moves through.

The shift between seasons can feel abrupt here. A week of warm weather may suddenly give way to cold mornings and heavy dew.

## LAUNCESTON, TASMANIA (INLAND, COLDER)

### JANUARY

Summer production is solid in sheltered Launceston gardens, but the season is narrower than at the coast. Tomatoes and beans perform well in good sun, though unseasoned gardeners often underestimate how much wind exposure slows growth here. Zucchini and squash do better than heat-sensitive crops in inland wind.

Capsicums and eggplants struggle in most years unless the summer is unusually warm. They are expensive to fail on, so only commit to them if you have a truly sheltered, north-facing bed or greenhouse protection.

Begin the autumn garden now, but be aware your frost window comes earlier than coastal Blackmans Bay. Sow carrots, beetroot, parsnips, turnips, kale, broccoli and cabbage while the soil is still warm enough for quick germination. Do not delay. Lettuce seed germinates poorly in Launceston heat. Chill it or shade it entirely until late February.

The inland location means wind is your constant opponent. Mulch heavily to anchor soil and conserve moisture. Deep morning watering works better than frequent shallow wetting, which only encourages fungal disease in damp Tasmanian air.

### FEBRUARY

Nights cool noticeably by February and the growing season's confidence begins to shift. This is the month where inland gardeners feel the difference from coastal gardens most acutely.

Plant out brassica seedlings quickly. They have a narrower window to establish before the serious April frosts arrive. Broccoli, cabbage and kale should be in the ground by mid-month at the latest. Continue sowing lettuce, Asian greens, silverbeet and beetroot while soil warmth remains.

Tomatoes and beans peak this month, but harvest regularly rather than waiting for ideal specimens. Wind-stressed plants finish faster than they would elsewhere, so succession harvesting keeps them productive longer.

By late February, the light quality shifts perceptibly. Mornings are sharper and the sun's angle changes enough for experienced gardeners to feel the approach of autumn clearly.

### MARCH

Autumn moves faster here than at the coast. Frosts are possible even mid-month in exposed positions, and they become increasingly likely by month's end, especially in wind-facing gardens or low pockets.

Plant garlic immediately. There is no room for delay. Sow spinach, peas and broad beans early in the month while soil warmth remains. Asian greens do well if sown early. Root crops should be in the ground by the second week or there is not sufficient warmth for proper establishment.

Remove summer crops promptly once they fail. The Launceston growing season rewards timing above all else. A crop lingering two weeks past its peak attracts slug damage and mould that will persist into winter.

Mulch thoroughly around young seedlings and new plantings. Frosts in Launceston come suddenly and with little warning. Protection becomes your best insurance against losing an entire season's establishment work.

## BRISBANE, QUEENSLAND (SUBTROPICAL)

### JANUARY

This is the tail end of wet summer. Growth is lush but the heat and humidity test plants badly. Tomatoes are often diseased by now, cucumbers produce but require water constantly, and beans struggle with fungal pressure in damp warm conditions.

Zucchini, pumpkin and sweet potato thrive in this heat and moisture, though fungal leaf disease can still appear on stressed plants. The key is air movement. Poor ventilation amplifies every disease problem.

The autumn garden is already underway here, not beginning. Sow cool-season crops in late January for establishment through the dry, cool months ahead. Lettuce, Asian greens, broccoli, cabbage, peas and root crops need to be in the ground by February. The subtropical dry season approaches and it is your growing season, not summer.

Water consistently despite the heat. Inconsistent watering causes far more problems than the heat itself. Mulch to prevent soil from baking hard during dry spells. The shift from wet to dry can be sudden and plants need established depth of soil moisture to survive the transition.

### FEBRUARY

The wet season weakens and the dry approaches. This is arguably Brisbane's most important planting month because you are establishing everything that will carry you through the cooler, drier months when growth truly flourishes.

Transplant cool-season seedlings: lettuce, Asian greens, broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, kale. Direct sow root crops and peas while the soil is still warm enough for quick germination. The faster these crops establish the better they perform during the dry months ahead.

Summer crops begin to fail now. Clear exhausted tomatoes, beans and tender plants promptly. Do not leave diseased foliage in the garden. Winter crops benefit from cleared space and diseased plant removal improves next summer's disease pressure dramatically.

Prepare beds richly for the dry months. Compost, aged manure and mulch matter tremendously because dry-season growth demands consistency and depth of moisture.

### MARCH

The dry season has clearly arrived. Heat and humidity drop noticeably, days shorten perceptibly and the garden enters its productive season. This is when Brisbane gardens come alive properly.

Continue planting cool-season seedlings and direct sowing. Lettuce, Asian greens, root crops, peas, brassicas all flourish now with the approaching cool weather and reliable dryness. Succession sowing begins again. A little every two weeks produces far better results than one heavy planting.

Warm-season crops are nearly finished. Clear them decisively. The space is valuable for autumn and winter crops that will produce far more heavily through the dry months than summer crops ever will.

Water deeply and mulch heavily for the dry months. Brisbane rainfall stops reliably by late March in most years. What you establish in March must carry moisture through months of dryness. Deep watering in March is worth far more than frequent shallow watering in August.

---

**Each location's voice reflects its actual growing reality: Blackmans Bay has coastal moderation but narrow seasons. Launceston is inland cold and wind-shaped. Brisbane flips the calendar—summer is hostile, autumn through winter is the real growing season.**
